# The Seven Questions Audit

Every business must answer seven questions. The book's benchmark: a great
company gets all seven right; getting five or six right may be survivable;
fewer means likely failure regardless of market size. The cautionary case is
the 2000s cleantech bubble — companies riding an enormous, real market
(energy) failed because they had no good answer to most of these.

Score each **pass / borderline / fail** with a one-line justification. Be
strict: founders grade themselves generously, so an advisor must not.

## 1. Engineering — the 10x question

*Can you create breakthrough technology (or a breakthrough method) instead of
an incremental improvement?*

- Pass: at least 10x better than the closest substitute on the dimension the
  customer buys on — faster, cheaper, or qualitatively new.
- Fail signals: "20% better," "cheaper by a third," "same thing but nicer
  UX." Incremental improvements are invisible to customers and defenseless
  against incumbents.

## 2. Timing — the now question

*Is now the right time to start this particular business?*

- Pass: a specific, recent unlock (new technology, regulation change,
  platform shift, cost curve crossing a threshold) explains why this works
  today and didn't three years ago.
- Fail signals: "the market is finally ready" without a mechanism; or a slow,
  linear improvement curve (cleantech solar efficiency inched forward — no
  moment of takeoff).

## 3. Monopoly — the market-share question

*Are you starting with a big share of a small market?*

- Pass: a definable beachhead — a few thousand concentrated users with an
  intense need — that the company can own outright, plus a concentric-circle
  expansion path.
- Fail signals: "1% of a trillion-dollar market"; a market defined by
  intersecting adjectives to fake uniqueness; no articulable first market at
  all. See `monopoly-and-competition.md`.

## 4. People — the team question

*Do you have the right team?*

- Pass: founders with real history together, complementary and technical
  where the problem demands it, full-time, mission-picked rather than
  fashion-picked.
- Fail signals (the book's cleantech tells): founders who present like
  salespeople rather than builders; cofounders who met at a networking event
  last quarter; key people part-time or remote-by-convenience from the cap
  table. See `foundations-and-team.md`.

## 5. Distribution — the sales question

*Do you have a way not just to create your product but to deliver it?*

- Pass: one identified channel where customer lifetime value clearly exceeds
  acquisition cost, matched to the product's price point.
- Fail signals: "the product sells itself"; a price point in the dead zone
  (too low to fund a salesperson, too niche for mass marketing); "we'll go
  viral" without a built-in sharing mechanic. See `distribution.md`.

## 6. Durability — the ten-year question

*Will your market position be defensible 10 and 20 years into the future?*

- Pass: a concrete answer to "what stops a well-funded copycat — or an
  incumbent, or a lower-cost country's industry — in year five?" Moats
  compound: proprietary tech + network effects + scale.
- Fail signals: the plan depends on subsidies, a temporary regulatory gap, or
  being first; competitors' roadmaps ignored (solar startups ignored that
  Chinese manufacturers would make the same panels cheaper).

## 7. Secret — the hidden-truth question

*Have you identified a unique opportunity that others don't see?*

- Pass: the business rests on a specific belief about nature or people that
  is true, important, and rejected or unnoticed by almost everyone —
  stateable in one sentence.
- Fail signals: the founding premise is something everyone already agrees
  with ("AI is big," "sustainability matters" — the book calls cleantech's
  version a matter of social conventions, not a secret). Conventional
  truths make competition, not companies. See `secrets-and-optimism.md`.

## Presenting the audit

Render as a table:

| # | Question | Verdict | Why (one line) |
|---|---|---|---|

Then expand only the fails and borderlines: for each, either a concrete route
to a pass or an honest statement that the flaw looks structural. Close with
the count — "N of 7" — and let that number, not optimism, drive the verdict.
