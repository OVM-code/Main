# Foundations, Team, and Culture

**Thiel's law: a startup messed up at its foundation cannot be fixed.**
Founding choices — cofounders, equity, board, culture — are like a country's
constitution: nearly impossible to amend later. Audit these before strategy,
because no strategy survives a broken foundation.

## Cofounders

- Choosing a cofounder is like a marriage; cofounder conflict is as ugly and
  costly as divorce. The test is **shared history**: how long have they
  known each other, and have they worked through hard things together before
  this company? Founders who met at a networking event and incorporated a
  month later are a red flag regardless of résumés.
- Complementary skills matter, but prehistory matters more — technical
  brilliance can be hired; trust can't.

## Ownership, possession, control

Misalignment between three distinct roles causes most internal blowups:

- **Ownership** — who legally holds equity (founders, employees, investors).
- **Possession** — who actually runs the company day to day.
- **Control** — who formally governs (the board).

Advisor checks:

- **Board size: keep it small** — three is ideal, never more than five for a
  private company. Every added seat adds a possible misalignment; a huge
  board exercises no real oversight at all.
- **Full-time or gone.** Everyone building the company should be full-time
  and on equity. Part-timers, remote-by-cap-table advisors, and consultants
  are misaligned by construction: they're positioned to collect near-term
  value, not build long-term value. (Deliberate exceptions exist — lawyers,
  accountants — but they're vendors, not builders.)
- **CEO pay: low cash.** The book's rule of thumb from VC experience: a
  funded-startup CEO drawing a high salary (the cited threshold: $150k/year)
  is a bearish signal — a well-paid CEO defends the status quo; a cash-poor,
  equity-rich CEO is aligned with making the whole thing valuable. CEO pay
  also sets the ceiling and the culture for everyone else's compensation.
- **Equity over cash for everyone.** Cash is the present; equity is the
  future. People who prefer salary over meaningful equity are telling you
  their time horizon. Equity grants will be unequal and must be — but keep
  individual grants confidential, because published grant tables read as
  unfair to everyone.

## Recruiting — why does the 20th employee join?

The first few hires join for equity and adventure. The hard question is the
20th: *why would a talented person join you rather than take a bigger salary
and more prestige at a giant company?* Bad answers: the stock options alone
(some company's are always better), the perks (big companies beat you on
perks), "smart people" and "hard problems" (every company claims those).
Good answers are specific to the mission and the team: "you'll work on this
problem, which matters for this reason, with these particular people —
and no one else is doing it." If the founder can't give that answer, the
mission isn't definite enough yet.

## Culture — the mafia, not the perks

- Culture is not ping-pong tables and catered lunch; it's what the company
  *is*. The strongest startups feel like a shared conspiracy around the
  company's secret — the "PayPal mafia" worked because its people were
  genuinely alike in what they cared about and stayed bound after the exit.
- Hire people who are excited about *this specific mission*, not generically
  impressive people; a team of mercenary all-stars dissolves under pressure.
- From the outside, total dedication to a mission looks cult-like. The book
  treats that as a feature: the opposite of a cult is a consulting firm —
  no mission, interchangeable people, hourly loyalty.

## One thing per person

Give every employee exactly **one** primary responsibility, distinct from
everyone else's. Not primarily for focus — to **eliminate internal
competition**: when two people own the same turf, they fight each other
instead of external rivals, and internal rivalry is where startup drama comes
from. When advising on org design, look for overlapping mandates first.
