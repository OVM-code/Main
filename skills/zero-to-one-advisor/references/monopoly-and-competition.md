# Monopoly and Competition

The book's central economic claim: **competition and capitalism are
opposites.** Under perfect competition, profits are competed away to zero;
only a monopoly — a company so good at what it does that no other firm offers
a close substitute — captures lasting value. "Monopoly" here means earned
dominance through superiority, not rent-seeking or regulatory capture.

## Why founders misread their own market

Both monopolists and competitors lie about their position, in opposite
directions:

- **Monopolists disguise their monopoly** by defining their market as huge
  ("we're a tiny player in global advertising/tech/commerce") to avoid
  scrutiny. Google framed itself as an advertising or technology company,
  not as the owner of search.
- **Competitors exaggerate their uniqueness** by defining their market as
  the intersection of narrow categories ("the only British-food restaurant
  in this neighborhood"). The relevant market is what the customer actually
  chooses between — for a restaurant, all nearby restaurants; for an app,
  everything else competing for the same use of time or money.

**Advisor move:** re-derive the market definition from the customer's real
alternatives, not the founder's category. If uniqueness survives *that*
definition, it's real.

## The four characteristics of monopoly

A durable monopoly usually combines several of:

1. **Proprietary technology — the 10x rule.** The core capability must be at
   least an order of magnitude better than the closest substitute on the
   dimension customers care about, because a 2–3x improvement reads as
   marginal and won't overcome switching inertia. Ways to get 10x: invent
   something new, improve something existing by 10x, or offer a radically
   better integrated design (as Apple did with the iPad).
2. **Network effects.** The product gets more valuable as more people use
   it — but network-effect businesses must start in markets so small the
   initial network is dense (Facebook began with one campus). "It'll be
   valuable once everyone is on it" is not a go-to-market plan.
3. **Economies of scale.** High fixed costs, near-zero marginal cost —
   software is the extreme case. Service businesses that must hire linearly
   with revenue scale poorly and rarely monopolize.
4. **Branding.** Real but the weakest to build on alone; a brand must wrap
   an underlying substantive advantage. "The Apple of X" without Apple's
   technology and supply-chain edge is theater.

## Start small and monopolize

- **Wrong question:** "How do we get 1% of a $100B market?" A small slice of
  a huge market means undifferentiated competition and zero pricing power.
- **Right question:** "What is the smallest market we can dominate
  completely?" PayPal started with eBay power sellers (~20k people), Amazon
  with books, Facebook with Harvard. The ideal beachhead: a small,
  concentrated group of users with an intense need and few or no
  alternatives.
- **Then scale in concentric circles:** expand from the beachhead into
  adjacent, slightly broader markets, in a sequence planned from day one
  (Amazon: books → media → everything).
- **Don't disrupt.** Framing yourself as a disruptor means defining yourself
  by the incumbents you fight — it invites their full attention and courts
  competition. Better to sidestep the fight entirely: create where there is
  no incumbent to provoke.

## Last mover advantage

First mover advantage is a tactic, not a goal — being first only matters if
you can hold the position. Most of a company's value lies in cash flows a
decade or more away, so the question is not "who gets there first" but "who
takes the last great step in the market and then enjoys years of monopoly
profits." Escape velocity matters more than launch order. When evaluating
growth-stage claims, discount current growth rates and ask whether the
business will still be dominant and durable in 10–15 years.

## War and peace

Rivalry makes people copy their competitors' moves and fight over scraps
(the book's examples: Microsoft vs. Google eroding both; startups cloning
each other's features). Two prescriptions:

- If a fight is unavoidable and winnable quickly and decisively, strike hard
  and end it.
- If it isn't, merge, exit, or move — as PayPal and X.com merged rather than
  burn each other down. Pride in the fight is not a strategy.
