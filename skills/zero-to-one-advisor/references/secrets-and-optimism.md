# Secrets, Definite Optimism, and the Power Law

Three frameworks that govern *where ideas come from*, *how to plan*, and
*how to prioritize*. Use this file for idea generation, planning philosophy,
and any "what should I focus on" question.

## Secrets — every company is built on one

A **secret** is a truth that is important, knowable with effort, and believed
by almost no one. Three categories of truth:

- **Conventions** — easy truths everyone knows. Companies built on them
  ("food delivery is convenient") face maximal competition.
- **Secrets** — hard truths you can discover. Every great company is built
  on exactly one: Airbnb saw unused rooms plus willing hosts where everyone
  else saw strangers-are-dangerous; PayPal saw that email could move money.
- **Mysteries** — truths that can't be known. Not actionable.

Why people stopped looking for secrets: incrementalism (rewarded for small
steps), risk aversion (being wrong about a secret is embarrassing),
complacency (comfortable positions don't need secrets), and "flatness" (the
assumption that in a globalized world, someone smarter would already have
found it). All four are habits, not evidence — the book's position is that
many secrets remain, and fields nobody is forced to standardize (nutrition
is its example) are full of them.

**Where to look:** two kinds — secrets of nature (undiscovered physical
science) and **secrets about people** — what people don't know about
themselves, or what they hide because it's taboo. The generative questions:

- "What are people not allowed to talk about?"
- "What is forbidden or taboo?"
- "What valuable company is nobody building?"

**Advisor use:** demand the founder state their secret in one sentence. If
the sentence is a convention, the business will be a competition. Also apply
the disclosure rule: a secret should be told only to those who need to know
— a company is a conspiracy to change the world around its secret, not a
press release.

## Definite vs. indefinite optimism

Four attitudes toward the future, from crossing *optimism/pessimism* with
*definite/indefinite* (whether the future can be planned):

| | Definite | Indefinite |
|---|---|---|
| **Optimist** | The future will be better **because we will design and build it** (the book's model: US mid-20th century — Apollo, interstates). | The future will be better but I can't say how — so keep options open, diversify, iterate (its diagnosis of the modern US: finance over engineering, process over substance). |
| **Pessimist** | The future is bleak and known — brace for it. | The future is bleak and unknowable — extract and consume now. |

Only **definite optimism** builds new things. Its business corollaries:

- **A bad plan beats no plan.** Iteration, MVPs, and A/B tests refine an
  existing design; they never generate the design. Rewrite "we'll iterate to
  product-market fit" as "here is the specific future we intend to build,
  and these are the milestones."
- **The anti-lean caveat.** Lean methodology optimizes within what exists;
  zero-to-one products are bold plans executed, not survey results. Use
  feedback to steer a definite plan, not to substitute for one.
- **Companies with definite plans are undervalued** by an indefinite world
  that only prices optionality — which is exactly why a founder who commits
  to a plan has an edge.
- **Founders should refuse to sell when acquirers knock, if** their definite
  plan shows more value ahead than the offer prices in (Zuckerberg's 2006
  refusal of Yahoo's $1B is the book's example). An offer only dominates
  when the plan has run out.

## The power law

Returns are not normally distributed — the best single outcome in a venture
fund outperforms the entire rest of the fund combined. Consequences:

- **For companies:** one market, one channel, one product decision will
  matter more than everything else combined. Find it and concentrate on it.
  Diversified effort inside a startup is indefinite optimism in disguise.
- **For founders personally:** you cannot diversify yourself. "Keep your
  options open" as a life strategy guarantees mediocrity at many things. The
  question is not "is this basket safe?" but "will this basket — this
  company, this market, this skill — be one of the outliers?"
- **Joining vs. founding:** owning 0.01% of an outlier beats owning 100% of
  a median startup. Advise people weighing offers to judge the company's
  power-law position, not their percentage.

**Advisor use:** whenever the user presents a list — of features, channels,
markets, priorities — force-rank it and cut to one. The deliverable of any
advising session is "the one thing," with everything else explicitly
subordinated.
